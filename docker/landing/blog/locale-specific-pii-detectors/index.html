The case for locale-specific PII detectors.

Most PII detectors are built around a comforting fiction: that a piece of personal data looks the same everywhere. Strip the formatting and a national ID is just digits, a phone number is just digits, an order reference is — also just digits.

Digits don't carry meaning. Context does.

A French NIR is fifteen digits. A German Steuer-ID is eleven. A US SSN is nine. On their own these are just numbers that happen to fall in a length range, and a context-free matcher will cheerfully flag an invoice total or a tracking number as a national identifier.

The fix isn't a stricter regex. It's reading the neighbourhood:

  • The label next to the value (Steuernummer:, N° de sécurité sociale).
  • The checksum the format defines — a real NIR validates; a random run of digits usually doesn't.
  • The language and country the surrounding text is written in.

Why false positives are a real cost

A detector that over-fires trains people to ignore it. Redact the order total as if it were a tax ID and the team turns the rule off. Now you've lost the true positives too. Precision isn't a nicety — it's what keeps the wall switched on.

And the false negatives are worse

The mirror image is quieter and more dangerous. A locale you didn't model is a locale you don't detect. A detector tuned only for US formats will wave a Polish PESEL straight through. "We scan for PII" becomes "we scan for American PII," and nobody notices until the data is already gone.

Locale-specific detectors cost more to build — a rule, a checksum, and a keyword set per country. But the alternative isn't a simpler detector. It's a detector that's confidently wrong in both directions.
